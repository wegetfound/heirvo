# Living Archive — Design Rationale

## Why search-first wins for an archive

Family video archives have a permanent search problem: you remember a moment, not a file. Grandma's birthday. The pancake-wedding story. The camping trip where Dad lost the keys. Once you have hours of recovered tape, browsing thumbnails is hopeless — but typing the half-sentence you remember is everything. The home screen is therefore a single Spotlight-style input, large and centered, with the rest of the page falling away into negative space. Recently recovered discs sit below as quiet text rows — a library catalog, not a Netflix grid.

## What's distinctive

The transcript is the protagonist; the video is the supporting actor. On the Watch screen the video sits in a small column and the transcript dominates as beautifully-set serif prose with monospace timestamps in the gutter. Click any line, the playhead seeks. The Disc Detail page reads like a Notes document: drop-cap opening, soft topic pills extracted from the transcript, scene markers as a sidebar of contents. Nothing screams. Everything invites slow reading.

## Interaction principles

Type-to-find from anywhere ("/" focuses search). Frosted Vision-Pro surfaces with warm radial ambient lighting suggest depth without ornament. Newsreader serif for content, Inter for chrome, JetBrains Mono for time — tabular numerals everywhere a timestamp appears.

## Tradeoffs

Text-first is hostile to users who want to *browse visually*. There is intentionally no grid of disc covers and no auto-playing previews. The bet: for a non-technical, emotionally invested audience who already knows what they're looking for, search-and-read beats scroll-and-scrub every time.
